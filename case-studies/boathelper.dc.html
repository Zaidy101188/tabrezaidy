BoatHelper: getting a boat fixed without the word-of-mouth guesswork

A mobile marketplace that turns "who do I even call?" into a structured task: post the job, compare vetted marine specialists on price and rating, agree in chat, and track the work to done.

The problem

Marine repair runs on referrals. An owner with a barnacle-fouled hull or a rough-running engine asks around the marina, gets two or three names, and has no real way to compare them — no consistent pricing, no verified credentials, no record of who actually finishes on time. A specialist, meanwhile, has no steady channel of qualified leads: just calls that may or may not turn into paid work.

Both sides needed the same thing: a shared, structured description of the job — what boat, what's wrong, what it should cost, and where and when — that either side could act on with confidence.

The approach

I designed BoatHelper around a single object: the task. An owner registers a boat, then raises a task against it — category, description, budget range, a map pin for the repair location, target dates, and photos of the damage. That task becomes the unit everything else hangs off: offers from craftsmen, the chat thread, the eventual agreement, and the progress record.

On the dashboard, price data across categories (varnishing, engine, electricals, hull work) is surfaced as a trend chart, so an owner posting a new task has a rough sense of a fair price before a single offer comes in. Craftsmen are presented as a comparable list — specialty, rate, certification, distance, and review count — rather than a wall of profiles to dig through individually.

The process, screen by screen

How a job moves from "something's wrong" to "done"

01 · Home

A dashboard that answers "where do things stand?" first

Open, in-progress, and done counts sit at the top, ahead of anything else, because that's the question an owner opens the app to answer. Below it: the boats on file, live market pricing by repair category, the owner's own open tasks with their offer counts, a shortlist of nearby craftsmen, and a feed of other owners in the same marina community.

02 · Create task

Turning "the engine sounds wrong" into something a craftsman can quote

The task form asks for exactly what a specialist needs to give a real quote: which boat, a task category, a plain-language description, a budget range, start and completion dates, and photos. Location is set by dropping a pin on an embedded map rather than typing an address, since most repair sites are a slip or mooring number, not a street.

An owner can save the task as a draft and come back to it, or publish it straight to the open-task pool where craftsmen can send offers.

03 · Craftsman marketplace

Comparing specialists the way you'd compare anything else

Every craftsman card carries the same five facts in the same order — specialty, hourly rate, star rating with review count, certification badge, and distance — plus an availability status and years of experience. Keeping the fields identical across the list is what makes it a comparison rather than a directory: an owner can scan rate against rating without opening a single profile.

04 · Task lifecycle

One record from open to done, not a chat thread that trails off

Every task carries a status — open, in progress, or done — and the same three tabs filter the list everywhere it appears. Once an owner accepts an offer, the task becomes an agreement: service details, start and end dates, the repair coordinates, and the photos stay attached to it for the life of the job.

An in-progress task shows the assigned craftsman, a progress indicator, the agreed price and estimated completion time, and direct Chat and Call actions — then a Mark as complete / Review pair once the work is actually done. Nothing about the job's status lives only in someone's memory of a phone call.

05 · Chat

Keeping the conversation attached to the job

Chat opens directly from a craftsman's card or an active agreement, never as a separate inbox disconnected from the task it's about. Attachments carry over the same way they do in task creation, so a photo of a part or a revised quote lands in context instead of a generic messaging screen.

The outcome

The result is a single task object that both sides can trust: an owner always knows whether a job is unanswered, being worked, or finished, and a craftsman sees a real brief — boat, budget, location, dates, photos — before ever writing a quote. Price trends and comparable craftsman cards replace guesswork with a frame of reference on both sides of the deal.

The system was designed to extend cleanly: the same task/offer/agreement structure covers any category of marine work without new screens, just new category tags on an existing flow.
